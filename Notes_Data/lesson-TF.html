Lesson: TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency)

Introduction

In the previous lesson, we explored the Bag-of-Words (BoW) model and its ability to convert text into numerical representations using word frequency vectors. However, BoW has a critical limitation: it treats all words equally, regardless of their importance in distinguishing between documents.

For example, common words like "the" and "is" appear frequently in all texts, but they do not contribute much to understanding the meaning of a document. The TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) technique addresses this issue by assigning higher weights to important words while down-weighting frequent but unimportant words.

TF-IDF is widely used in search engines, text classification, and information retrieval to enhance text representation by weighting words based on their importance.

Understanding TF-IDF

TF-IDF stands for Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency, and it’s a fundamental technique used in Natural Language Processing and Information Retrieval to evaluate how important a word is to a document in a collection or corpus.

It’s especially useful for tasks like document ranking, keyword extraction, and search engines.

What is TF-IDF?

TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) is a statistical measure used to evaluate how important a word is in a specific document relative to a larger collection of documents, known as a corpus.

It balances two key factors:

  • Term Frequency (TF): Measures how often a word appears in a document. The more frequent the word, the more important it may seem for that document.
  • Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): Discounts the importance of words that appear in many documents across the corpus, giving higher weight to words that are more unique or informative.

Together, TF-IDF highlights words that are frequent in a document but rare across the corpus, helping identify the most meaningful terms in a given context.

The TF-IDF score is calculated as:

\[ w_{t,d} = tf_{t,d} \times idf_t \]

where:

  • \(w_{t,d}\) is the TF-IDF weight of term ( t ) in document ( d ).
  • \(tf_{t,d}\) is the term frequency in the document.
  • \(idf_t\) is the inverse document frequency across all documents.

Term Frequency (TF)

The Term Frequency \((tf_{t,d​})\) represents the number of times a term \(t\) appears in a document \(d\). However, using raw counts can introduce bias toward longer documents that naturally contain more words. To address this, normalization techniques are applied.

Raw Count

The simplest approach—just counts the number of times the term appears:

\[ $tf_{t,d}​=\text{count}(t,d)$ \]

Log Normalization

To reduce the disproportionate influence of very frequent terms, logarithmic scaling is used. This approach dampens the effect of large term frequencies while preserving relative importance.

The log-weighted term frequency is defined as:

\[ tf_{t,d} = \begin{cases} 1 + \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d)) & \text{if } \text{count}(t, d) > 0 \\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

This formula ensures that:

  • Words appearing more frequently still have higher importance.
  • The rate of growth slows down logarithmically, preventing extremely frequent words from dominating. This weighting helps reduce the impact of very frequent terms.

Example

Suppose the word "disaster" appears 0 times, then tf = 0.

If it appears 1 time, then \(tf = 1 + log₁₀(1) = 1\).

If it appears 10 times, then \(tf = 1 + log₁₀(10) = 2\).

If it appears 100 times, then \(tf = 1 + log₁₀(100) = 3\), and so on.

Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) is a measure used to downweight common words that appear in many documents across the corpus. The idea is that terms occurring in many documents are less useful for distinguishing one document from another.

The formula for IDF is:

\[ idf_t = \log_{10} \left( \frac{N}{df_t} \right) \]

where:

  • \(N\) is the total number of documents.
  • $tf_ $ is the number of documents containing the term ( t ).

Words that appear in almost every document—such as "the", "is", or "and"—have high document frequency (\(df_t\)), resulting in a low IDF. In fact, if a word appears in all documents, its IDF becomes:

\[ idf_t = \log_{10} \left( \frac{N}{N} \right)=\log_{10}(1)=0 \]

Example: Shakespeare’s Plays

Assume we have a collection of 37 plays written by Shakespeare. We can compute the Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) for various words based on how many plays each word appears in:

Word Document Frequency (\(df\)) IDF \(\log_{10}\left( \frac{37}{df} \right)\)
Romeo 1 1.57
Salad 2 1.27
Fool 36 0.012
Good 37 0.00

Interpretation:

  • "Romeo" appears in only one play, so its IDF is high (1.57), making it a strong indicator of that specific document.
  • "Salad" is rare, showing up in just two plays, so it also has a relatively high IDF (1.27).
  • "Fool" appears in 36 of the 37 plays, so its IDF is close to zero, indicating it's too common to be distinctive.
  • "Good" appears in all 37 plays, resulting in an IDF of 0, meaning it contributes no value in distinguishing one play from another.

This example highlights how IDF helps identify unique or significant terms while downweighting common, less informative words.

Example Calculation

Step 1: Compute Term Frequency (TF)

Suppose we analyze a document where "Romeo" appears 10 times. Using log normalization:

\[ tf_{\text{Romeo},d} = 1 + \log_{10}(10) = 2 \]

Step 2: Compute Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Since "Romeo" appears in only one of 37 documents:

\[ idf_{\text{Romeo}} = \log_{10} \left( \frac{37}{1} \right) = 1.57 \]

Step 3: Compute TF-IDF

\[ w_{\text{Romeo},d} = 2 \times 1.57 = 3.14 \]

This high weight means that "Romeo" is a significant term in this document.

Implementing TF-IDF in Python

We can compute TF-IDF using TfidfVectorizer from sklearn.feature_extraction.text.

Step 1: Import Required Libraries

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

corpus = [
    "Antony appears in Julius Caesar and Antony and Cleopatra.",
    "Brutus appears in Julius Caesar and Hamlet.",
    "Caesar appears in Julius Caesar, Hamlet, and Othello."
]

Step 2: Create and Apply the TF-IDF Vectorizer

vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer()
X = vectorizer.fit_transform(corpus)

Step 3: Display the Vocabulary

print("Vocabulary:", vectorizer.get_feature_names_out())

Output:

Vocabulary: ['antony' 'appears' 'brutus' 'caesar' 'cleopatra' 'hamlet' 'julius' 'othello']

Step 4: Convert Text into TF-IDF Matrix

print("TF-IDF Matrix:\n", X.toarray())

Applications of TF-IDF

TF-IDF is a foundational technique in Natural Language Processing (NLP), widely used for extracting meaningful insights from text. Its ability to highlight important terms makes it valuable in many real-world scenarios:

  1. Search Engines: TF-IDF helps rank documents based on how relevant they are to a user's query by scoring terms that are both frequent in a document and rare across the corpus.
  2. Document Similarity: By converting documents into TF-IDF vectors, we can compute how similar two texts are using distance metrics like cosine similarity—useful for clustering or recommendation systems.
  3. Plagiarism Detection: TF-IDF can highlight unique terms or unusual overlaps in phrasing, helping detect copied or highly similar content across large document collections.
  4. Text Classification: TF-IDF improves model performance by emphasizing discriminative words and reducing noise from common, uninformative terms—making it ideal for spam detection, topic categorization, and sentiment analysis.

Comparing Bag-of-Words (BoW) and TF-IDF

Both Bag-of-Words (BoW) and TF-IDF are popular methods for representing text as numerical features in NLP. While they share some similarities, they differ in how they treat word importance and document context.

Feature Bag-of-Words (BoW) TF-IDF
Considers frequency Yes Yes
Handles common words effectively No Yes
Differentiates important words No Yes
Keeps word order No No

Summary:

  • BoW simply counts word occurrences without considering how common or rare the words are across documents.
  • TF-IDF enhances this by reducing the weight of common words and highlighting unique, informative ones—making it more suitable for tasks like search and classification.

Conclusion

TF-IDF improves upon the Bag-of-Words model by reducing the weight of common words and highlighting important ones. This makes it a powerful tool for text representation in NLP tasks such as search engines, document classification, and text similarity.
