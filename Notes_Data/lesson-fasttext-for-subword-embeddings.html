Lesson: FastText for Subword Embeddings

Introduction

Word embeddings like Word2Vec and GloVe give each word its own vector, like a unique address in space that captures its meaning. But what happens when we come across rare words or out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words (words that our model hasn’t seen before?) These models often struggle because they treat each word as a single, unbreakable piece.

FastText addresses this limitation by incorporating subword information. It decomposes words into character n-grams and represents each word as a sum of its subword vectors. This method enables the model to generate meaningful embeddings for rare or even misspelled words, making it particularly effective for morphologically rich and multilingual contexts.

This lesson outlines the structure and mechanisms of FastText, emphasizing its advantages in handling linguistic variability and its applicability to multilingual natural language processing tasks.

  2. Analyze the advantages of FastText in representing rare and OOV words compared to traditional word embedding methods.
  3. Identify real-world applications of FastText in multilingual and low-resource natural language processing scenarios.

How FastText Handles Rare and Out-of-Vocabulary Words

FastText improves on earlier word embedding models by addressing a key limitation: the inability to handle rare or unseen words. Traditional models like Word2Vec and GloVe break down when encountering such cases, but FastText solves this by modeling words as combinations of smaller character-level units. To understand this solution, we’ll first look at the limitations of whole-word embeddings, and then explore how FastText uses subword modeling to overcome them.

Limitations of Whole-Word Embeddings

Let’s start by understanding the problem. Traditional models like Word2Vec and GloVe treat words as whole units. If a word wasn’t in the training data—like a rare word or a typo—the model doesn’t know what to do with it. It can’t give that word a vector, which is a big problem in situations like:

  • Rare words: Words that show up only a few times in the data.
  • New or misspelled words: Words the model has never seen, like "helo" instead of "hello."
  • Languages with lots of word forms: Some languages, like Arabic or Turkish, create many versions of a word by adding prefixes or suffixes.

FastText solves this by looking at the smaller pieces that make up a word. Instead of treating a word as one big unit, it breaks it into parts, which lets it guess the meaning of new or rare words based on those shared pieces.

Subword Modeling with FastText

FastText enhances word representation by breaking each word into smaller, overlapping sequences of characters. This subword modeling approach allows the system to capture shared linguistic patterns across words and construct embeddings even for those it has never seen before. This process is implemented using character n-grams, which we explore next.

Character N-Grams: Breaking Words into Pieces

FastText doesn’t treat a word as a single thing. Instead, it breaks each word into smaller chunks called character n-grams. These are short sequences of letters, usually 3 to 6 characters long. For example, let’s take the word where:

  • If we use 3-grams (sequences of 3 letters), FastText breaks where into:
    • <wh, whe, her, ere, re>

FastText adds special symbols, < at the start and > at the end, to show where the word begins and ends. This helps it tell the difference between the start of a word (like <wh) and the middle (like whe).

Each of these n-grams gets its own vector—a kind of mini-address in space. FastText then sums up all these mini-vectors to create the final vector for the whole word.

Mathematical Formulation

Let us formalize how FastText constructs word embeddings using subword information. Suppose we have a word \(w\), and let \(G_w\) denote the set of all its character n-grams (e.g., <wh, whe, her, etc.). Each n-gram is associated with a vector \(z_g\) , where is the embedding dimension. FastText computes the vector representation of the word \(w\), denoted \(w_w\), by summing the vectors of its n-grams:

\[ v_w = \sum_{g \in G_w} z_g \]

Where:

  • \(G_w\): The set of n-grams extracted from word
  • \(z_g\): The embedding vector corresponding to n-gram
  • \(v_w\): The resulting word embedding for word

This formulation is powerful because it naturally encodes morphological similarity. Words like run, runner, and running will share overlapping subword units (e.g., run, unn, nni), resulting in similar overall word vectors, even if some of the words are rare or previously unseen. By leveraging subword information, FastText improves generalization and robustness, especially in low-resource or noisy text environments.

Illustrative Example: Embedding Construction for play

Let’s demonstrate how FastText constructs a word embedding using character-level 3-grams.

Step 1: Generate Character N-Grams

FastText augments the word boundaries and then extracts all 3-character n-grams. For the word play:

\[ G_{\text{play}} = \{\texttt{<pl},\ \texttt{pla},\ \texttt{lay},\ \texttt{ay>}\} \]

Step 2: Retrieve N-Gram Vectors

Assume the model has learned the following one-dimensional vectors (for simplicity):

\[ \begin{aligned} z_{\texttt{<pl}} &= 0.5 \\ z_{\texttt{pla}} &= 0.9 \\ z_{\texttt{lay}} &= 0.7 \\ z_{\texttt{ay>}} &= 0.4 \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Compute the Word Embedding

The final embedding for the word play is computed as the sum of its n-gram vectors:

\[ v_{\text{play}} = \sum_{g \in G_{\text{play}}} z_g = z_{\texttt{<pl}} + z_{\texttt{pla}} + z_{\texttt{lay}} + z_{\texttt{ay>}} = 0.5 + 0.9 + 0.7 + 0.4 = 2.5 \]

In practice, $$( z_g \in \mathbb^d )$, and the sum is performed element-wise across vector dimensions.

Insights

If another word like playing shares n-grams such as pla, lay, or ay, their embeddings will partially overlap, resulting in similar vector representations, even if one of the words was rare or unseen during training.

FastText vs. Word2Vec: Comparative Analysis

Let’s compare FastText to Word2Vec to see how they’re different. Word2Vec is great, but it has some limitations that FastText improves on. Here’s a simple table to break it down:

Feature Word2Vec FastText
Vocabulary coverage Only words seen during training Can handle new or unseen words
Representation One vector per whole word Adds up vectors of word pieces
Handling rare words Struggles—no vector for rare words Works well using shared word pieces
Understanding word forms Doesn’t look at word structure Understands prefixes and suffixes
Multilingual support Not as strong Great for many languages

FastText is better because it uses subword sharing. For example, if the model knows the word play, it can still understand playing or even a typo like plaing by looking at their shared pieces. This makes FastText really good for languages with lots of word forms, messy text (like social media posts with typos), or smaller datasets where some words might be missing.

Illustration of OOV Handling: Handling New Words with Subword Units

Let’s see FastText in action with an example. Imagine your model was trained on the word where, but now it sees a new word, wheres, which it hasn’t seen before. Word2Vec would give up because wheres isn’t in its vocabulary. But FastText can handle it!

FastText breaks wheres into its 3-grams:

  • <wh, whe, her, ere, res, es>

Many of these pieces—like <wh, whe, her, and ere—are the same as in where. Since FastText already learned vectors for these n-grams from other words, it can add them up to create a vector for wheres. This way, even though wheres is new, FastText can still guess its meaning based on its parts.

Applications of FastText in Multilingual NLP

FastText is particularly effective in multilingual natural language processing due to its ability to model subword information, which is especially valuable in languages with rich morphology. Many languages, such as German, Finnish, or Arabic, create numerous word forms through affixation. For example, the German word Haus (“house”) can appear as Häuser (“houses”) or Häuschen (“little house”). FastText is able to generalize across these forms by recognizing and leveraging shared subword units.

Why FastText Excels in Multilingual Settings

  1. Learns morphological patterns across languages
    FastText captures roots, prefixes, and suffixes that are common across many languages, making it well-suited to model linguistic variation.
  2. Handles unseen words in any languageEven when encountering rare or new words, FastText can construct embeddings by combining known subword vectors, enabling strong generalization.
  3. Wide language supportFastText provides pre-trained word vectors for over 157 languages, making it a valuable tool for:
    • Code-switching: Handling texts that mix multiple languages.
    • Low-resource language processing: Working effectively with languages that lack large annotated corpora.
    • Cross-lingual applications: Supporting tasks like multilingual retrieval or translation through aligned embeddings.

Practical Applications

FastText is used in a variety of multilingual NLP systems and pipelines, including:

Machine Translation

FastText embeddings support translation models by providing reliable vector representations for morphologically complex or rare words, especially in low-resource settings.

import fasttext
import fasttext.util

# Download and load pre-trained vectors (e.g., German and English)
fasttext.util.download_model('de', if_exists='ignore')  # German
fasttext.util.download_model('en', if_exists='ignore')  # English

de_model = fasttext.load_model('cc.de.300.bin')
en_model = fasttext.load_model('cc.en.300.bin')

# Compare similarity of "Haus" and "house"
from numpy import dot
from numpy.linalg import norm

def cosine_similarity(vec1, vec2):
    return dot(vec1, vec2) / (norm(vec1) * norm(vec2))

vec_de = de_model.get_word_vector("Haus")
vec_en = en_model.get_word_vector("house")
print(f"Similarity between 'Haus' and 'house': {cosine_similarity(vec_de, vec_en):.4f}")

Named Entity Recognition (NER) Because FastText can infer representations for unseen words based on subword patterns, it is effective in recognizing entities such as names, locations, and organizations—even if they were not present during training

# Assume we're using English pre-trained vectors
model = fasttext.load_model('cc.en.300.bin')

# New, unseen name
print("Vector for 'McGreen':")
print(model.get_word_vector("McGreen"))

Even if "McGreen" never appeared in training, FastText builds its vector using fragments like "McG", "Gre", "ree", "een".

Spelling Correction and Normalization

FastText can suggest corrections for misspelled words by finding vector-nearest neighbors in the embedding space, enabling systems to recover intended words based on form similarity.

# Misspelled word
misspelled = "enviroment"

# Find nearest neighbors in vector space
print("Suggestions for 'enviroment':")
print(model.get_nearest_neighbors(misspelled))

This returns words like "environment", "environments", etc., based on vector similarity—not just character edit distance—making corrections context-aware and linguistically informed.

Technical Summary: Model Training Overview

FastText builds on the Skip-Gram method from Word2Vec, but with a twist. Let’s break down how it learns:

  • Training pairs: It looks at pairs of words in a sentence, like a center word and a context word (e.g., in "I like to run," the pair might be "like" and "to").
  • Negative sampling: It also creates fake pairs by picking random words that aren’t in the context, to teach the model what doesn’t belong.
  • Word representation: Instead of using one vector for the center word, FastText breaks the word into n-grams and adds up their vectors to represent the word.
  • Goal: Make the center word’s vector (from its n-grams) similar to the context word’s vector for real pairs, and different for fake pairs.

The similarity between an n-gram vector \(z_g\) (from the center word) and a context word vector \(v_c\) is measured using a scoring function:

\[ \sigma(z_g^T \cdot v_c) \]

Here, \(\sigma\) is a function that turns the dot product into a probability, helping the model decide how similar the vectors are. By learning vectors for n-grams instead of whole words, FastText can build embeddings for any word, even ones it hasn’t seen before.

Conclusion

In this lesson, we explored how FastText solves a big problem in traditional word embeddings: dealing with rare and new words. By breaking words into smaller pieces called character n-grams and adding up their vectors, FastText can understand words it has never seen before. This makes it especially great for messy text, languages with lots of word forms, and working with multiple languages at once.

FastText is a simple but powerful upgrade to Word2Vec, giving us word representations that are more flexible, reliable, and useful across different languages and tasks. It’s widely used in research and real-world applications, from translation to spelling correction, making it a fantastic tool for anyone working in NLP.
