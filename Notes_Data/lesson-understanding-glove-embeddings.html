Lesson: Understanding GloVe Embeddings

Introduction

After exploring prediction-based models like Word2Vec, we now turn to GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation) a model that returns to the count-based family of vector semantics but with important innovations. GloVe aims to combine the strengths of count-based methods and predictive embeddings by focusing on capturing global word co-occurrence statistics in a principled way.

In this lesson, we will explore how GloVe works, how it builds meaningful vector representations using a co-occurrence matrix, and how it differs from models like Word2Vec in terms of both philosophy and implementation.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. Describe the GloVe model and its training objective.
  2. Explain how co-occurrence counts are used to learn word embeddings.
  3. Compare GloVe to Word2Vec in terms of model type, optimization, and representation.

Overview on GloVe

GloVe (Global Vectors) is a count-based model that learns word embeddings from a word-word co-occurrence matrix. Unlike Word2Vec, which learns embeddings by predicting context from a word or vice versa, GloVe explicitly factorizes a matrix of word co-occurrence counts.

Its key intuition is:

Words that co-occur frequently with similar sets of words should have similar vector representations.

GloVe uses global corpus statistics (the co-occurrence counts across the entire corpus) rather than local predictions (one context window at a time). Despite being count-based, GloVe produces dense, low-dimensional vectors (e.g., 100–300 dimensions) similar to those learned by Word2Vec, capturing both semantic and syntactic relationships.

Co-occurrence Matrix

GloVe starts with building a term-term matrix where:

  • Each row is a target word
  • Each column is a context word
  • Each cell \(( X_{ij})\) is the number of times word \(( j)\) appears in the context of word \(( i )\), typically within a fixed window (e.g., ±4 words around the target word).

The context window size determines how far GloVe looks for co-occurrences. A window of ±4 means GloVe counts words up to 4 positions before and after the target word, often weighted by distance (e.g., closer words contribute more to the count).

Example:

Consider the following co-occurrence matrix, where each cell represents how often a given word appears in the context of another:

computer data pie sugar
cherry 2 8 442 25
strawberry 0 0 60 19
digital 1670 1683 5 4
information 3325 3982 5 13

We can see that cherry and strawberry frequently co-occur with words like pie and sugar, while digital and information tend to appear with data and computer. These co-occurrence patterns form the basis for building word vectors. Words with similar distributional profiles—such as cherry and strawberry—end up with similar embeddings. This reflects the core idea of distributional semantics: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

GloVe's Objective Function

GloVe creates word embeddings by looking at how often words appear together in a text and using that information to understand their relationships. Let’s go through this process step by step to make it easy to understand.

Understanding Co-occurrence Probabilities

GloVe’s clever idea is to use the ratios of probabilities to figure out how words are related. First, it counts how often words appear near each other. Imagine we have a word \(i\), and we want to know how many times any word appears in its context (like within a few words before or after it). We call this total count \(X_i\), and it’s the sum of all counts in row \(i\) of the co-occurrence matrix, written as:

\[ X_i = \sum_k X_{ik} \]

Next, GloVe calculates the probability that a specific word \(k\) appears in the context of word \(i\). We call this probability \(P_{ik}\), and it’s the number of times \(k\) appears with \(i\), divided by the total count \(X_i\):

\[ P_{ik} = \frac{X_{ik}}{X_i} \]

Now, GloVe compares these probabilities for two words \(i\) and \(j\) with respect to word \(k\) by looking at their ratio:

\[ \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \]

This ratio tells us how much more (or less) likely word \(k\) is to appear with word \(i\) compared to word \(j\). For example, if \(i = \text{ice}\), \(j = \text{steam}\), and \(k = \text{solid}\), the ratio \(\frac{P_{\text{ice, solid}}}{P_{\text{steam, solid}}}\) would be a big number because "solid" appears much more often with "ice" than with "steam." This helps GloVe understand that "ice" and "solid" are more closely related.

Building Simple Relationship with Vectors

To capture these relationships, GloVe gives each word two vectors: one called the input vector \(w_i\), and another called the context vector \(\tilde{w}_j\). It also adds two small numbers, called bias terms \(b_i\) and \(\tilde{b}_j\), to adjust for things like how common a word is. GloVe’s goal is to make the following equation true:

\[ w_i^T \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j = \log X_{ij} \]

This equation is called a log-bilinear model. Let’s break that down: the "bilinear" part comes from multiplying the two vectors together (using a dot product $(w_i^T \tilde_j $), and the "log" part comes from using the logarithm of the co-occurrence count \(\log X_{ij}\). The logarithm makes big and small counts easier to work with, and the biases help fine-tune the result.

Creating Loss Function to Learn

GloVe learns by trying to make its predictions as close as possible to the actual co-occurrence counts. It does this by using a loss function, which measures how far off its predictions are. The loss function GloVe uses is:

Here’s what each part means:

  • \(|V|\): The total number of unique words in the vocabulary.
  • \(w_i^T \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j\): GloVe’s prediction for the log co-occurrence.
  • \(\log X_{ij}\): The actual log co-occurrence count we want to match.
  • \(f(X_{ij})\): A special weighting function to make sure frequent word pairs don’t take over.

This loss function adds up the errors for all word pairs, squares them (to make sure errors are always positive), and adjusts them with the weighting function.

Using a Weighting Function for Balance

The weighting function \(f(X_{ij})\) helps GloVe focus on the most useful word pairs. Very common pairs (like "the" and "is") happen a lot but don’t tell us much about meaning, so GloVe gives them less importance. The weighting function is defined as:

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} (\frac{x}{x_{\text{max}}})^\alpha & \text{if } x < x_{max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
  • \(x_{\text{max}}\): A limit, usually set to 100, to keep the weighting from getting too big.
  • \(\alpha\): A number, often 0.75, that decides how much to focus on less frequent pairs.

This weighting makes sure GloVe pays more attention to word pairs that happen often enough to be meaningful, but not so often that they’re just noise, helping create better embeddings.

Intuition Behind GloVe

The goal is to learn word vectors where differences in the vector space encode meaningful relationships, such as analogies:

\[ w\_{\text{king}} - w_{\text{man}} + w_{\text{woman}} \approx w_{\text{queen}} \]

This results from the structure imposed by co-occurrence statistics and the model’s optimization objective, not from hard-coding such rules. For example, the result of the expression \(\vec{\text{king}}-\vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}}\) is a vector close to \(\vec{\text{queen}}\). The embedding model thus seems to be extracting representations of relations like MALE-FEMALE, or COMPARATIVE/SUPERLATIVE, as shown:

Relational properties of the GloVe vector space, shown by projecting vectors onto two dimensions. (a) \(\vec{\text{king}}-\vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}}\) is close to \(\vec{\text{queen}}\). (b) offsets seem to capture comparative and superlative morphology.

Strengths and Limitations of GloVe

Strengths

  • Captures fine-grained semantic relationships using global co-occurrence statistics.
  • Provides interpretable embeddings, making it easier to understand and visualize word relationships.
  • Performs well on a variety of NLP benchmarks, including word similarity and analogy tasks.
  • Suitable for pretraining embeddings on large, static corpora.
  • Embeddings are consistent and reproducible, independent of random initialization.

Limitations

  • Requires building a large word-word co-occurrence matrix, which is memory- and compute-intensive.
  • Less suitable for dynamic or streaming data, as retraining is non-trivial.
  • Struggles with rare or out-of-vocabulary words not well represented in the co-occurrence matrix.
  • Embedding quality is sensitive to hyperparameters, such as:
    • Context window size
    • Minimum frequency thresholds
    • Weighting functions (e.g., the x-max parameter)

GloVe vs. Word2Vec

Although GloVe and Word2Vec both produce word embeddings and can solve tasks like analogies and word similarity, they learn those embeddings in very different ways.

Word2Vec is a prediction-based model. It learns word meanings by guessing: “If I know this word, can I predict the nearby words?” or the opposite, “Can I guess the missing word from the nearby ones?” It does this while reading the text one window at a time, learning from each short phrase as it appears.

In contrast, GloVe is a count-based model. Instead of guessing context words like Word2Vec, it builds a huge table first, counting how often each pair of words appears near each other. It then tries to compress that giant table into vectors that still reflect the original relationships.

Think of it like this:

Concept Word2Vec GloVe
Learns from Local context (one phrase/window) Entire corpus (global co-occurrence)
Method Trains a neural net to predict Factorizes a word-word count matrix
Goal Predict missing words Match co-occurrence patterns
Analogy Like learning by doing flashcards Like learning by studying a full map
  • Word2Vec is like practicing many small language puzzles, learning embeddings by predicting words in short contexts. This makes it flexible and adaptive but can miss broader patterns in the data.
  • GloVe is like looking at the big picture—a map of how all words relate to each other across the entire corpus. This global view helps GloVe capture broader statistical patterns but requires more memory to store the co-occurrence matrix.

Practical Implications:

  • Training Speed: Word2Vec can start producing embeddings quickly as it processes the corpus sequentially, while GloVe requires building the co-occurrence matrix first, which can be memory-intensive for large corpora.
  • Performance: GloVe often performs better on tasks requiring global semantic understanding (e.g., word analogy tasks), while Word2Vec (especially Skip-Gram) excels at capturing local syntactic patterns and rare word relationships.
  • Adaptability: Word2Vec can easily update embeddings by continuing training on new data, whereas GloVe typically requires rebuilding the co-occurrence matrix to incorporate new data.

Both approaches are powerful, but they approach the problem of word meaning differently: Word2Vec learns from what happens nearby, while GloVe learns from how often things happen everywhere.

Use Case Scenarios: GloVe vs. Word2Vec

GloVe is often chosen for tasks that benefit from a global understanding of language structure. Because it builds embeddings by analyzing co-occurrence statistics across the entire corpus, GloVe captures broader thematic relationships between words. This makes it especially effective for:

  • Sentiment analysis, where antonyms and polarity are encoded through co-occurrence patterns.
  • Semantic search, where understanding conceptual similarity across documents is essential.
  • Text classification, where embeddings serve as rich input features even with limited labeled data.

GloVe is preferred in situations where:

  • You have access to large, static corpora.
  • You want to pre-train embeddings once and reuse them across models.
  • Interpretability and consistency across runs are important.

Word2Vec, in contrast, is often favored for its flexibility and local contextual learning. It excels at tasks where capturing fine-grained usage patterns of words is essential, especially for:

  • Real-time or adaptive systems, where models need to update with new data (e.g., chatbots, user modeling).
  • Rare word handling, particularly with the Skip-Gram model, which learns well from limited context.
  • Language modeling or transfer learning, where embeddings are learned jointly with a larger architecture.

Word2Vec is typically preferred when:

  • You’re working with domain-specific or dynamic data.
  • You want context-aware embeddings that reflect local linguistic usage.
  • You’re training end-to-end models and embeddings are part of the optimization process.

Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned how GloVe offers a count-based alternative to predictive models like Word2Vec. Instead of predicting words, GloVe learns word vectors by factorizing a matrix of co-occurrence statistics, optimizing for meaningful semantic relationships via log-ratio constraints.

Though its training pipeline differs, the resulting vectors support many of the same powerful properties as Word2Vec—similarity, analogy-solving, and clustering—while offering a model grounded in global corpus structure.
