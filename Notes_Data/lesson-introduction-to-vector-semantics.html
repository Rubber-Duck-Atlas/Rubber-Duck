Lesson: Introduction to Vector Semantics

Introduction

In Week 1, we explored how machines understand and process human language, laying the foundation for core NLP concepts like tokenization and the distributional hypothesis. This week, we build on that understanding by introducing vector semantics, a powerful framework that allows us to represent words as points in a mathematical space.

In natural language, words can carry complex meanings, relationships, and nuances. However, computers do not inherently understand these meanings. How can we teach machines to represent and reason about the meanings of words in a way that supports intelligent processing? Vector semantics provides a solution by transforming words into mathematical vectors based on the contexts in which they appear. This allows algorithms to compare, cluster, and manipulate meanings numerically.

Understanding vector semantics is a foundational step toward building intelligent NLP systems. Whether for translation, sentiment analysis, or search, vector representations are at the heart of how modern models understand language.

This lesson explores how we can model word meaning as numbers in a high-dimensional space, how these representations help us compute similarity, and how they unlock powerful applications in NLP—from search engines to chatbots.

Understanding Vector Semantics and Its Importance

Vector semantics is a method of representing word meanings as numerical vectors in a high-dimensional space. This idea is based on the distributional hypothesis, which states:

"Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings."

For example, consider the unknown word "ongchoi" that appears in phrases like:

  • Ongchoi is delicious sauteed with garlic.
  • Ongchoi is superb over rice.
  • ...ongchoi leaves with salty sauces...And suppose that you had seen many of these context words in other contexts:
  • ...spinach sauteed with garlic over rice...
  • ...chard stems and leaves are delicious...
  • ...collard greens and other salty leafy greens

Seeing it used in ways similar to spinach, chard, or collard greens, we might guess that it’s a leafy green. This is how vector semantics models meaning—by looking at word usage patterns.

By capturing co-occurrence information from large text corpora, we can encode the meaning of a word based on its neighbors.

For example, consider the words "doctor" and "nurse". These words often appear in similar contexts like "hospital", "patient", and "treatment". When represented as vectors, "doctor" and "nurse" will lie close together in the semantic space.

Importance of Vector Semantics

Computers can’t interpret words symbolically like humans do. By representing words as vectors, we give machines a numerical way to:

  • Compare meanings: Words like "doctor" and "nurse" appear near "hospital" or "patient," so their vectors are similar.
  • Perform operations: Vectors enable mathematical tasks like measuring similarity or solving analogies.
  • Enable NLP: Applications like sentiment analysis and search rely on these representations.

This transformation turns language into a computable format, making vector semantics a cornerstone of modern NLP.

So, words that appear in similar contexts like doctor and nurse end up with similar vector representations. These patterns come from co-occurrence data in large corpora.

Capturing Semantic Relationships with Word Vectors

To build word vectors, we use large corpora and compute how frequently each word appears near others (within a sliding window or in entire documents).

There are two main types of vector representations:

1. Word-Document Vectors

In NLP, word-document vectors represent words as vectors capturing their frequency across different documents, such as Shakespeare's plays, with the following table and graph providing a simplified two-dimensional visualization. Each word is represented by a vector that shows how often it appears in each document. For instance, consider the Shakespeare term-document matrix:

Word As You Like It Twelfth Night Julius Caesar Henry V
battle 1 0 7 13
good 114 80 62 89
fool 36 58 1 4
wit 20 15 2 3

Each word becomes a vector based on its frequency in each play. For example, "battle" is [1, 0, 7, 13]. Notice how "battle" is rare in comedies like As You Like It and Twelfth Night but common in historical plays like Julius Caesar and Henry V. This helps us see patterns—words that appear in similar documents are often related in meaning.

Here’s a visual of this idea:

A spatial visualization of the document vectors for the four Shakespeare play documents, showing just two of the dimensions, corresponding to the words battle and fool. The comedies have high values for the fool dimension and low values for the battle dimension.

2. Word-Word Co-occurrence Vectors

Another way is to count how often words appear near each other in the same text. For example, we might count how often "cherry" appears near "pie," "sugar," "computer," or "data" and so on. To illustrate this concept, consider the following term-term co-occurrence matrix:

Word computer data pie sugar
cherry 2 8 442 25
strawberry 0 0 60 19
digital 1670 1683 5 4
information 3325 3982 5 13

The numbers show how often the row word appears near the column word in a text corpus, typically within a small window (e.g., 5 words). For example:

  • Cherry and Pie: 442 means "cherry" appears near "pie" 442 times, indicating a strong link (e.g., "cherry pie").
  • Digital and Computer: 1670 shows "digital" appears near "computer" 1670 times, reflecting a frequent pairing.
  • Information and Data: 3982 indicates "information" appears near "data" 3982 times, a common combination.
  • Cherry and Computer: 2 suggests a weak connection, with only 2 co-occurrences.

This matrix helps reveal semantic relationships, like "cherry" and "strawberry" being similar (both near "pie" and "sugar"), while "digital" is unrelated.

Here’s a visual representation of "digital" and "information":

Aspatial visualization of word vectors for digital and information, showing just two of the dimensions, corresponding to the words data and computer.

This structure allows the model to learn that cherry and strawberry are similar (both appear near pie and sugar), while digital is not.

Similarity Computation: Cosine Similarity

Now that we have vectors, how do we compare words? We use a math tool called cosine similarity, which measures the angle between two vectors:

\[ \text{cos}(v, w) = \frac{v \cdot w}{\\|v\\| \\|w\\|} \]

Where:

  • $v \cdot w $ is the dot product
  • \(|v|\) and \(|w|\) are vector magnitudes
  • A value close to 1 means the words are very similar, while a value near 0 means they’re different. Let’s try it with some simplified vectors:

Example:

Let’s compare how close information is to cherry and digital:

Vectors:
cherry      = [442, 8, 2]
digital     = [5, 1683, 1670]
information = [5, 3982, 3325]

Using the cosine formula:

cos(cherry, information) ≈ 0.018
cos(digital, information) ≈ 0.996

Digital is much closer in meaning to Information than Cherry is. This makes sense, "digital" and "information" are closely related (think "digital information"), while "cherry" is quite different.

Dimensionality Reduction for Visualization

Word vectors often exist in high dimensions (50–300+), making visualization difficult. Techniques like t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) and PCA (Principal Component Analysis) reduce these to 2D while preserving key relationships. t-SNE emphasizes local structures, modeling pairwise similarities to cluster similar words effectively, while PCA takes a linear approach, projecting data onto principal components that capture the most variance.

In the next activity, you’ll explore pre-trained embeddings like GloVe and word2vec to visualize how semantically related words group in 2D space. GloVe uses global co-occurrence statistics to encode word relationships, while word2vec employs predictive methods (e.g., skip-gram) to learn representations from word contexts, capturing semantic similarities for visualization.

Applications of Word Vectors

Word vectors form the foundation of many natural language processing tasks by enabling models to capture the meaning of words through geometric relationships in high-dimensional space. Below are key applications that demonstrate their utility across different NLP scenarios.

1. Word Analogies

Word vectors can solve analogies using vector arithmetic. A well-known example:

\[ \text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} ≈ \text{queen} \]

The model computes:

vector("king") - vector("man") + vector("woman")

This arithmetic highlights how word vectors capture semantic relationships. The model identifies the word closest to the computed vector, demonstrating how concepts like gender or profession form consistent directional patterns in embedding space.

2. Sentiment Analysis

Word vectors allow sentiment classifiers to generalize beyond exact training words. For example:

  • Words like amazing, wonderful, and excellent cluster near good.
  • Words like awful, terrible, and horrible cluster near bad.

Even if a model hasn’t seen a new word during training, it can infer its sentiment from its proximity to known sentiment-bearing words in vector space.

3. Machine Translation

In machine translation, word vectors support the alignment of meanings across languages by placing semantically equivalent words close together in a shared multilingual embedding space. For example, the English word book and the French word livre may appear near each other in this space, reflecting their similar usage and meaning.

This alignment allows models to learn mappings between languages even without explicit word-to-word dictionaries. Vector-based translation systems, especially when trained on parallel corpora, can exploit these geometric relationships to identify accurate translations and support tasks like cross-lingual retrieval, bilingual lexicon induction, and zero-shot translation—where the model translates between language pairs it was not explicitly trained on.

4. Search Engines

Word vectors play a critical role in modern search engines by enabling semantic retrieval—the ability to match meaning rather than just exact keywords. When a user submits a query, the system converts it into a vector and retrieves documents whose embeddings lie close in vector space. This proximity reflects thematic or conceptual similarity, even when the words used are different.

Search engines have two main steps:

  • Retrieval: From a massive collection, identify a shortlist of documents whose vectors are close to the query vector.
  • Ranking: Further analyze and score these candidates using advanced models to present the most relevant results first.

For example, a search for “Romeo and revenge” might surface documents about Hamlet or Macbeth, not because they contain the same words, but because they share similar narrative themes captured by their vector representations. After retrieval, a ranking model refines the results by scoring them for relevance. This vector-based approach allows search systems to better understand intent and deliver results that reflect semantic alignment, improving the quality of information retrieval in both web and enterprise search settings.
